# Patient Living Guides — Clinical Editorial & Product Standard

**Project:** HOME VISITING ICU DOCTOR  
**Status:** implementation standard; owner/physician review required before clinical publication  
**Audience:** patients and family caregivers in India, with plain-language English and authored Marathi first; Google Translate remains an accessibility aid, not a substitute for clinical translation.

## 1. Purpose

The Health Library must help a person live as well as possible with illness—not merely list medicines or describe a diagnosis. Each guide should translate sound clinical knowledge into safe, specific actions that fit a real day, a real home, local food, family support, work, affordability and the patient's own priorities.

The model is person-centred and supports self-management. It does not replace clinical assessment, prescribe an individual treatment plan, or promise that a checklist can prevent every complication.

## 2. Required structure for every disease-specific guide

Every guide must contain these sections, in this order:

1. **What the diagnosis means:** explain the condition without frightening language; distinguish a symptom, a risk factor, a test result and a confirmed diagnosis.
2. **What matters to this person:** ask what the patient wants to keep doing (walking to the temple, cooking, sleeping comfortably, returning to work, caring for grandchildren, etc.). Functional goals matter alongside numbers.
3. **A realistic day plan:** morning, meals, medicines, movement/rehabilitation, rest, monitoring, evening and sleep. Give small actions, not an impossible timetable.
4. **Medicine safety:** name/purpose, dose and timing only when an approved patient-specific prescription exists; adherence barriers; missed-dose instructions must be medicine-specific; interactions, storage, refill and review.
5. **Food and fluid:** familiar Indian foods and practical substitutions; state exactly when a restriction applies and what measurement or clinician instruction supports it. Never invent a universal water, salt, protein, fruit or vegetable ban.
6. **Movement and rest:** safe starting point, pacing, falls prevention, rehabilitation and stop/seek-help symptoms. No one-size-fits-all exercise target for unstable or frail patients.
7. **Home monitoring:** which measurement, which device, how to measure, when to record, how often, what trend matters, and who should receive the record. Explain what a measurement cannot diagnose.
8. **Strictly avoid:** only well-supported, condition-relevant hazards. Explain the reason and the safer alternative. Do not create fear-based blanket prohibitions.
9. **Three levels of escalation:** emergency now; same-day clinician contact; routine follow-up. Red flags must be condition-specific and must not delay emergency services.
10. **Family/caregiver actions:** consent, communication, safe transfers, medication reconciliation, skin/oral care, device hygiene, respite and caregiver burden.
11. **Low-cost Indian practicality:** household measures, locally available foods, public/low-cost care routes where verified, heat/power/travel constraints, language and literacy accessibility.
12. **Questions for the next visit:** a short checklist that helps patients participate in shared decisions.
13. **Sources, review and limitations:** direct primary/guideline links, organisation, publication/update date where available, date checked, author/reviewer and any uncertainty.

## 3. Universal daily-life framework (individualise before use)

- **Morning:** symptom check-in; only clinician-requested measurements; personal hygiene; prescribed medicines; breakfast plan.
- **Midday:** meal and fluid plan; safe movement or rehabilitation in tolerable blocks; rest breaks; avoid prolonged immobility when movement is safe.
- **Afternoon:** one practical health task (medicine refill, appointment, exercise set, wound/skin check, record review) and one meaningful non-medical activity.
- **Evening:** review new symptoms and medicine supply; prepare tomorrow's doses only using a verified list; social connection and enjoyable activity.
- **Night:** regular sleep routine; safe access to toilet, lighting and mobility aid; device/oxygen/catheter/tube checks only as prescribed and trained.

The patient should not be made to feel that a successful day means completing every item. Start with one or two realistic goals; adjust for fatigue, disability, acute illness and caregiver availability.

## 4. Medication safety rules

- Maintain one reconciled list of prescriptions, OTC products, supplements, herbal products, allergies and previous adverse reactions.
- Never share prescription medicines or use another person's dose.
- Never stop, start, double, crush, split or open a medicine based on generic internet advice. Modified-release and enteric-coated products need pharmacist confirmation before alteration.
- A missed-dose instruction must be specific to the actual medicine; do not publish “take it as soon as remembered” as a universal rule.
- Do not self-start leftover antibiotics, oral steroids, injections, sedatives or anticoagulants.
- Ask about affordability, swallowing difficulty, side effects, confusing schedules and refill access. These are solvable adherence barriers, not moral failures.
- Review polypharmacy, renal/hepatic dosing, orthostatic symptoms, falls risk and drug interactions in older adults and people with multimorbidity.

## 5. Evidence and wording policy

Use a hierarchy: current Indian national/state guidance and specialty-society guidance where applicable; WHO; NICE; high-quality systematic reviews; major academic centres (Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic); MSD Manual; then other reputable patient resources. A major centre's article is useful education, not automatically a formal guideline.

Label recommendations as:
- **Strong safety advice:** broad consensus and low risk (e.g., do not share prescriptions; seek emergency help for stroke/heart-attack warning symptoms).
- **Condition-specific recommendation:** only apply when the diagnosis, treatment or measurement criteria are met.
- **Individualised decision:** depends on laboratory results, organ function, frailty, pregnancy, medicines, goals or clinician action plan.
- **Evidence uncertain / evolving:** say so plainly; do not turn it into a strict rule.

Do not copy US-specific emergency numbers, insurance rules, food brands or screening schedules into Indian patient advice. Localise units, emergency access, food examples and health-system pathways. If a verified Indian pathway is unavailable, say so rather than inventing one.

## 6. Condition roadmap (build and review one module at a time)

Priority order for a Pune/Indian primary-care and home-visiting audience:

1. Type 2 diabetes: meals, glucose monitoring, hypoglycaemia, foot/eye/kidney protection, sick-day rules, insulin storage and injection safety.
2. Hypertension and cardiovascular risk: accurate home BP technique, adherence, salt advice without myths, stroke/MI warning signs.
3. Heart failure and coronary disease: personal weight/fluid action plan, breathlessness, oedema, diuretic safety, activity and emergency thresholds.
4. COPD and asthma: inhaler technique, trigger reduction, written exacerbation plan, oxygen safety, when breathlessness is urgent.
5. CKD and dialysis: stage- and lab-specific food/fluid advice, potassium, dialysis attendance, vascular access care, medicine safety.
6. Stroke recovery: swallowing, aspiration prevention, mobility, pressure injury, communication, mood, caregiver support and secondary prevention.
7. Frailty, falls, dementia and delirium: medication review, home safety, nutrition, sleep, cognition, caregiver respite and sudden-change red flags.
8. Fever and common infections: hydration only as appropriate, safe symptom relief, antibiotic stewardship, dehydration/sepsis warning signs and high-risk groups.
9. Diarrhoea/vomiting and dehydration: oral rehydration where appropriate, red flags, older-adult/child risk and when home care is unsafe.
10. Osteoarthritis and chronic pain: movement, pacing, sleep, safe analgesic use, function and non-drug options.
11. Depression, anxiety and chronic illness: practical coping, social connection, treatment access, medicine safety and self-harm escalation.
12. Post-discharge/ICU recovery: discharge reconciliation, nutrition, mobility, wounds/devices, follow-up, delirium, caregiver training and readmission warning signs.

## 7. Quality gates before an article is visible

- Clinical source links resolve and support the statements they accompany.
- Each red flag is clinically defensible and distinguishes emergency from same-day review.
- No unsupported dose, treatment change, laboratory threshold or universal restriction.
- English and Marathi authored versions are checked for equivalent meaning; machine translation is labelled and the original remains available.
- Mobile reading, keyboard access, PDF export, print and article-level translation are tested.
- A named clinician/editor and review date are visible. Unreviewed drafts remain labelled as drafts.
- New content does not silently rewrite previously owner-approved articles.

## 8. Source set used for the initial daily-living guide

- WHO — [Self-care for health and well-being](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/self-care-health-interventions): self-care complements, not replaces, health services.
- WHO — [Therapeutic patient education](https://www.who.int/europe/teams/ncd-management/therapeutic-patient-education): structured, person-centred self-management education.
- Cleveland Clinic — [Medication management](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/medication-management): medication list, storage, reminders, adverse effects and pharmacist/clinician review.
- Cleveland Clinic — [Chronic Care Clinics](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/departments/chronic-care): personalised goals, medication review and quality-of-life focus.
- Mayo Clinic — [Exercise and chronic disease](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/fitness/in-depth/exercise-and-chronic-disease/art-20046049): adapt activity to condition and clinician advice; small amounts can help.
- MSD Manual Consumer — [Healthy living](https://www.msdmanuals.com/home/healthy-living): practical prevention, movement, nutrition, sleep and social wellbeing.
- MSD Manual Consumer — [When to see a doctor](https://www.msdmanuals.com/home/special-subjects/making-the-most-of-health-care/when-to-see-a-doctor): warning symptoms and limits of generic triage lists.

## 9. Governance

This document is an editorial and product standard, not an approved clinical guideline. The newly added “Your day still belongs to you” / “आजार असला तरी दिवस आपलाच” article is a general educational foundation. Disease-specific advice must be written and checked separately, with the patient's condition and context made explicit. Keep the release branch unmerged until code, clinical content, CI and the desktop/mobile preview have been reviewed.
