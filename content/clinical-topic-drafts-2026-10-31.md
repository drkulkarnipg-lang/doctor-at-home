# Patient education drafts — physician review required

**Status:** DRAFT ONLY · NOT PUBLISHED · NOT IMPORTED INTO THE PUBLIC ARTICLE LIBRARY  
**Prepared:** 31 October 2026  
**Audience:** Patients and family caregivers in India  
**Scope:** Plain-language education and preparation for a clinician conversation. These drafts do not prescribe medicines, doses, oxygen settings, glucose targets, fluid limits or individual treatment. They must be clinically reviewed, locally adapted and translated by a qualified reviewer before publication.

The current authored patient-education dataset contains nine detailed guides. These four drafts address the recorded gaps in COPD, cancer palliative care, diabetes and cirrhosis. This file is deliberately kept outside the public article data and publishing workflow.

## Draft 1 — COPD: understanding breathlessness and staying safer at home

**Standfirst:** COPD is a long-term lung condition. A practical plan, correct inhaler use and early attention to a flare-up can help you remain as active as possible.

### What COPD means

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) makes it harder for air to move out of the lungs. A person may have a long-lasting cough, cough up phlegm, wheeze, feel tired or become breathless during ordinary activity. Tobacco smoke is an important cause, but second-hand smoke, smoke from cooking fuels, and workplace dust or fumes can also damage the lungs. Asthma and COPD can sometimes occur together.

Symptoms alone do not confirm COPD. A clinician assesses the history and examination and may arrange spirometry, a breathing test that measures airflow. Bring previous reports and the names of every inhaler or nebulised medicine to the appointment.

### Your daily plan

- Use each inhaler exactly as prescribed. Ask the doctor, nurse or pharmacist to watch you use it and correct your technique. Ask whether a spacer is suitable for your device.
- Do not stop a regular inhaler, increase a dose, start steroid tablets or antibiotics, or change home oxygen on your own. Ask for a written plan explaining what to do if symptoms worsen.
- Avoid tobacco and vaping. Ask for help to stop smoking; it is useful even after many years of smoking. Reduce exposure to other people's smoke, dusty work and indoor cooking smoke where possible.
- Keep physically active within the limits set by your clinician. Ask whether pulmonary rehabilitation is available.
- Ask which influenza, pneumococcal and COVID-19 vaccines are appropriate for your age and health conditions.
- Keep a current medicine list and note your usual breathing level, walking ability and any recent flare-ups.

### Recognising a flare-up

A flare-up is a noticeable worsening of your usual breathing or cough. You may become more breathless, cough more, or notice a change in the amount or appearance of phlegm. Follow the written action plan agreed with your treating team and contact them promptly. Do not use leftover antibiotics or steroid tablets as a substitute for review.

### When to seek urgent help

Severe breathlessness at rest, inability to speak because of breathlessness, blue or grey lips, new confusion or marked drowsiness, collapse, or chest pain needs urgent assessment. In India, call 112 for a life-threatening emergency. Do not wait for an online reply.

### Questions to take to your doctor

1. Has COPD been confirmed with spirometry, and what other conditions could explain my symptoms?
2. Can you check my inhaler technique and tell me which inhaler is regular and which is for relief?
3. What exact changes should trigger a same-day call, an urgent visit or emergency care?
4. Do I need a written flare-up plan, pulmonary rehabilitation or vaccination review?

**Sources for review**

- [WHO — Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), 10 June 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/chronic-obstructive-pulmonary-disease-%28copd%29)

## Draft 2 — Cancer palliative care: extra support alongside cancer treatment

**Standfirst:** Palliative care helps a person and family manage symptoms, worries and practical needs. It can be started early and may be provided while cancer treatment continues.

### What palliative care means

Palliative care is active care focused on quality of life. It addresses pain, nausea, poor appetite, tiredness, breathlessness, sleep problems and emotional distress. It can also support communication, family caregivers, practical concerns and spiritual needs. It is not a sign that the patient has been abandoned or that all cancer treatment must stop.

Palliative care may be provided alongside treatment intended to control or treat the cancer. It can be offered in a hospital, outpatient clinic or, where a suitable team and support are available, at home. The oncology team can explain which local services are accessible.

### How the team can help

The patient and family can ask for a review of symptoms, medicine side effects, nutrition, mobility, sleep, mood and caregiver strain. The team can help the patient describe what matters most, understand treatment choices and plan for likely changes. A care plan should state whom to contact during the day and after hours, and what to do if symptoms suddenly worsen.

### Conversations worth having

- What is the main aim of each current cancer treatment?
- Which symptoms can be treated now, and who should we call if they worsen?
- Would a palliative-care referral help alongside the oncology plan?
- What matters most to the patient when choosing between treatment benefits, burdens and time at home?
- Does the patient want to record care preferences or identify a trusted person to speak on their behalf if communication becomes difficult?
- What practical or emotional support is available for the family caregiver?

Palliative care and hospice/end-of-life services are related but not identical. The timing, eligibility and services available vary. Ask the treating team to explain the local options rather than assuming that a referral means cancer treatment is being withdrawn.

### When to seek urgent help

Severe breathlessness, major bleeding, sudden confusion, collapse, uncontrolled severe pain or another rapidly worsening symptom needs urgent clinical assessment. Follow the oncology team's emergency instructions; call 112 in India for a life-threatening emergency. Do not wait for an online response or change strong pain medicines without the prescribing clinician's advice.

**Sources for review**

- [National Cancer Institute — Palliative Care in Cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet)
- [WHO — Palliative care](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/palliative-care/)

## Draft 3 — Diabetes: everyday safety, medicines and follow-up

**Standfirst:** Diabetes care is individual. A clear plan for medicines, food, activity, monitoring and sick days is safer than copying another person's routine.

### Make the plan fit the person

Diabetes management depends on the type of diabetes, age, other health conditions, kidney and heart health, pregnancy where relevant, current medicines and the person's circumstances. Your doctor should agree on your own glucose goals and how often you need testing. Bring your glucose record, medicine packets and recent reports to review. Do not change a medicine dose because of a single reading or another person's advice.

### Everyday habits that help

- Take medicines as prescribed and ask what to do if a dose is missed, a meal is delayed or you cannot eat normally.
- Follow a meal plan that suits your health, culture, budget and activity. Avoid extreme diets or supplements advertised as a cure.
- Be physically active within the limits agreed with your clinician.
- Check your feet regularly. Look for blisters, cuts, redness, swelling, cracks or colour changes. Wear suitable footwear and avoid walking barefoot. Ask for help rather than cutting corns or calluses yourself.
- Attend the eye, kidney, blood-pressure, cholesterol and foot checks recommended for you.
- Keep a list of medicines, allergies and emergency contacts.

### Know the signs of low glucose

Low glucose may cause sweating, shaking, sudden hunger, a fast heartbeat, dizziness or confusion. Ask your clinician for a written plan that explains how to recognise and treat a low reading for your medicines and circumstances. If the person is unconscious, having a seizure or unable to swallow safely, do not put food, drink or tablets in the mouth. Call emergency services.

### Make a sick-day plan before you need it

Fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, poor food intake or dehydration can change glucose levels and medicine safety. Ask your clinician in advance which medicines need special instructions, when to check glucose or ketones, how to maintain fluids, and when to call or attend hospital. Do not stop insulin without individual medical advice. Persistent vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, severe weakness, confusion, breathing difficulty or repeated very high or low readings needs prompt clinical assessment.

### Questions to take to your doctor

1. What are my personal glucose targets and testing schedule?
2. Which symptoms or readings mean I should call the clinic urgently?
3. What is my written low-glucose plan?
4. What should I do with each medicine if I am vomiting, fasting or unable to eat?
5. Who should check my feet, eyes and kidney health, and when?

## Draft 4 — Cirrhosis: home monitoring, food safety and warning signs

**Standfirst:** Cirrhosis is scarring of the liver. Early disease may cause few symptoms, so follow-up and a plan for warning signs are important even when a person feels reasonably well.

### What cirrhosis means

Cirrhosis is long-term scarring that changes how the liver works. Causes include alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic fatty liver disease and chronic hepatitis B or C; more than one cause may be present. Some people feel well in the early stages. Later symptoms can include tiredness, itching, poor appetite, weight or muscle loss, easy bruising, yellow eyes or skin, swelling of the legs or abdomen, and changes in sleep, memory or alertness.

The treating team may use blood tests, imaging and other assessments to understand the cause, monitor complications and decide which reviews are due. Keep appointments even when symptoms are quiet.

### Food, drink and medicines

Nutrition needs differ from person to person and may change with the stage of liver disease. Ask for an individual plan from the treating doctor or dietitian. Do not start a severe protein restriction, fluid restriction or salt restriction unless your team has explained that it is appropriate for you. Some people need smaller, more frequent meals or a late-evening snack; ask what fits your condition.

Avoid alcohol. Avoid raw or undercooked shellfish, fish and meat, and unpasteurised milk products because infections can be serious in cirrhosis. Check with the treating clinician or pharmacist before taking over-the-counter painkillers, herbal or Ayurvedic products, supplements or a new medicine. Do not adjust water tablets or other prescribed medicines yourself.

### What to record at home

If your clinician asks, record weight, abdominal or leg swelling, temperature, appetite, bowel pattern, urine changes, sleep and alertness. Keep a current medicine list and recent reports together. Ask which changes need a same-day call and whom to contact outside clinic hours.

### Warning signs that need urgent assessment

Vomiting blood, black tarry stools, new or worsening confusion or unusual drowsiness, fever with new or worsening abdominal swelling, severe abdominal pain, rapidly worsening jaundice, severe breathlessness or collapse can signal a serious complication. Seek urgent medical care; call 112 in India for a life-threatening emergency. Do not wait for the next routine appointment or an online reply.

### Questions to take to your liver specialist

1. What is the likely cause of my cirrhosis, and can that cause be treated?
2. Which symptoms or home-record changes need a same-day call?
3. What food, salt, fluid and protein plan is right for me?
4. Which medicines, painkillers and supplements should I avoid or check first?
5. Which surveillance tests and specialist reviews are due for me?

## Mandatory physician-review checklist before any publication

- Confirm every clinical statement, warning sign and referral instruction against current Indian practice and the intended patient population.
- Review COPD inhaler, vaccination and exacerbation wording; do not add drug names, doses or oxygen settings without a separate approved clinical protocol.
- Review cancer palliative-care wording with an oncology/palliative-care clinician; distinguish palliative care from hospice and local service eligibility.
- Review diabetes wording for type 1, type 2, older adults, pregnancy, renal impairment and hypoglycaemia safety; do not add universal glucose targets or medicine changes.
- Review cirrhosis diet and emergency wording with a hepatology clinician; avoid blanket protein/fluid restrictions and ensure medication cautions are accurate.
- Verify the India emergency number, local referral pathways, home-care scope, accessibility and patient reading level.
- Obtain a qualified Marathi translation and independent clinical review of that translation. Do not publish machine-translated medical content as clinician-verified.
- Add a named clinical reviewer, review date and next review date only after the owner approves them.

**Publication gate:** No draft in this file is public, indexed, imported into the patient article dataset or approved for clinical use. Physician review and explicit owner approval are required before conversion into public article records.
